Overview

Formula One (F1) is the highest class of worldwide racing for open-wheel, single-seater formula racing cars run by Formula One Group and sanctioned by the Fédération Internationale de l'Automobile (FIA). The FIA Formula One World Championship has been one of the world's premier forms of motorsport since its inaugural running in 1950 and is often considered to be the pinnacle of motorsport. The word formula in the name refers to the set of rules all participant cars must follow. A Formula One season consists of a series of races, known as Grands Prix. Grands Prix take place in multiple countries and continents on either purpose-built circuits or closed roads.
A points scoring system is used at Grands Prix to determine two annual World Championships: one for the drivers, and one for the constructors—now synonymous with teams. Each driver must hold a valid Super Licence, the highest class of racing licence the FIA issues, and the races must be held on Grade One tracks, the highest grade rating the FIA issues for circuits.
F1 is owned by Liberty Media Corporation, acquired in 2017 for an estimated $8 billion, previously owned by British business magnate Bernie Ecclestone. The new ownership added five Grands Prix to the calendar: Miami, Las Vegas, Saudi Arabia, Qatar and Dutch.

History

Formula 1 (F1) began in 1950 as the highest level of international single-seater motor racing, with the first official World Championship race held at Silverstone in the United Kingdom. Over the decades, the sport evolved from dangerous, mechanically simple cars into highly advanced machines using cutting-edge aerodynamics and hybrid technology. Legendary drivers such as Juan Manuel Fangio, Ayrton Senna, Michael Schumacher, Lewis Hamilton, and Max Verstappen helped shape different eras of the sport through their dominance and rivalries. Teams like Ferrari, McLaren, Williams, Mercedes, and Red Bull became iconic names in motorsport history. Safety improved dramatically after several fatal accidents, especially following Senna’s death in 1994, leading to stricter regulations and safer track designs. F1 expanded globally, hosting races across Europe, Asia, the Middle East, the Americas, and Australia. Technological innovation has always been central to Formula 1, with many automotive advancements later influencing regular road cars. Today, Formula 1 is one of the world’s most popular sports, known for its speed, strategy, engineering, and intense competition.

An F1 season usually contains around 22–24 races, called Grands Prix, held in different countries throughout the year. During each race weekend, drivers compete in qualifying sessions to determine the starting order, followed by the main race on Sunday. Points are awarded based on finishing positions, with the winner receiving 25 points and lower positions receiving fewer points. Additional points may also be awarded for sprint races and the fastest lap under certain conditions.

The Drivers’ Championship is decided by the total number of points earned by each driver across the season. Similarly, the Constructors’ Championship is awarded to the team with the highest combined points from both of its drivers. At the end of the season, whoever has the most points becomes the world champion. Consistency, race wins, strategy, teamwork, and car performance all play major roles in determining the champions.

Conclusion

Formula 1 has grown into one of the most advanced and exciting sports in the world, combining speed, technology, strategy, and teamwork. With teams and drivers competing across many countries each season, the sport continues to attract millions of fans globally. The championship system rewards both individual driver skill and team performance, making every race important. As Formula 1 continues to evolve with new technology and regulations, it remains a symbol of innovation, competition, and excellence in motorsport.
